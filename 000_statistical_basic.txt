BASIC STATISTICAL CONCEPTS FOR PERFORMANCE BENCHMARKING

This summary uses 10 execution-time measurements, expressed in
milliseconds.

Samples: 9, 10, 11, 10, 10, 9, 11, 10, 9, 11 ms

N = 10 samples

1.  MEAN

The mean is the average execution time obtained from all measurements.

Mean = sum of samples / number of samples

In this example:

Sum = 9 + 10 + 11 + 10 + 10 + 9 + 11 + 10 + 9 + 11 = 100 ms

Mean = 100 / 10 = 10 ms

Interpretation: Our initial estimate of the average execution time is 10
ms.

In C++:

double mean = sum / measurements.size();

2.  DEVIATION FROM THE MEAN

To study how much the measurements vary, we calculate the difference
between each sample and the mean.

Sample Deviation from 10 ms 9 ms -1 ms 10 ms 0 ms 11 ms +1 ms 10 ms 0 ms
10 ms 0 ms 9 ms -1 ms 11 ms +1 ms 10 ms 0 ms 9 ms -1 ms 11 ms +1 ms

If we simply added these deviations, positive and negative values would
cancel each other out.

For this reason, the deviations are squared when calculating variance.

3.  VARIANCE

Variance measures how dispersed the samples are around the mean by using
squared deviations.

For each sample:

(sample - mean)^2

In this example:

Sample Deviation Squared deviation 9 -1 1 10 0 0 11 +1 1 10 0 0 10 0 0 9
-1 1 11 +1 1 10 0 0 9 -1 1 11 +1 1

Sum of squared deviations = 6

Using the formula used in our benchmark:

Variance = 6 / 10 = 0.6 ms^2

Interpretation: The larger the variance, the more dispersed the
execution-time measurements are.

IMPORTANT: Variance is expressed in squared units. If execution time is
measured in milliseconds, variance is expressed in ms^2.

In C++:

double variance = 0.0;

for (double measurement : measurements) { variance += (measurement -
mean) * (measurement - mean); }

variance /= measurements.size();

4.  STANDARD DEVIATION

The standard deviation is the square root of the variance.

Standard Deviation = sqrt(Variance)

In this example:

sqrt(0.6) ≈ 0.775 ms

Interpretation: The execution times have a typical dispersion of
approximately 0.775 ms around the 10 ms mean.

An important advantage over variance is that standard deviation is
expressed in the original measurement units: milliseconds.

Approximately:

                 mean
                   |
                   v

———|———|———|——— 9.225 10 10.775 <———> ±0.775 ms

If the measurements approximately follow a normal distribution, about
68% of the observations are expected to fall within one standard
deviation of the mean:

mean ± standard deviation

The 68% rule depends on the normal-distribution assumption and is not
guaranteed for every dataset.

5.  VARIANCE VS. STANDARD DEVIATION

Variance: - Measures dispersion. - Uses squared deviations. - In this
example: 0.6 ms^2.

Standard deviation: - Is the square root of variance. - Also measures
dispersion. - Uses the same units as the original measurements. - In
this example: approximately 0.775 ms.

Standard deviation is therefore usually easier to interpret when
analyzing benchmark results.

6.  STANDARD ERROR (SE)

Standard deviation describes how much the INDIVIDUAL measurements vary.

The Standard Error describes the uncertainty or expected variability of
the calculated MEAN as an estimate of the true average execution time.

SE = Standard Deviation / sqrt(N)

In this example:

SE = 0.775 / sqrt(10) SE ≈ 0.245 ms

Therefore:

Mean = 10 ms StdDev ≈ 0.775 ms SE ≈ 0.245 ms

Interpretation:

Standard Deviation: How much do individual execution-time measurements
vary?

Standard Error: How precisely have we estimated the average execution
time?

Because:

SE = StdDev / sqrt(N)

increasing the number of measurements generally decreases the Standard
Error.

Conceptually:

N = 10 -> relatively larger SE N = 100 -> smaller SE N = 1000 -> even
smaller SE

This is one reason why a reliable benchmark should execute the operation
multiple times instead of relying on a single measurement.

7.  95% CONFIDENCE INTERVAL

In our code, we use an approximation based on the normal distribution:

Margin of Error = 1.96 * SE

The value 1.96 is the critical value corresponding to approximately the
central 95% of a normal distribution.

For our example:

SE ≈ 0.245 ms

Margin ≈ 1.96 * 0.245 Margin ≈ 0.480 ms

Therefore:

95% CI ≈ Mean ± Margin

95% CI ≈ 10 ± 0.480 ms

Result:

[9.520 ms, 10.480 ms]

Practical interpretation: The confidence interval expresses the
uncertainty associated with our estimate of the mean execution time.

It does NOT mean that “95% of individual executions will take between
9.520 and 10.480 ms.”

The confidence interval concerns the estimated MEAN, not the
distribution of individual measurements.

In C++:

double standardError = stddev / sqrt(measurements.size()); double margin
= 1.96 * standardError;

cout << “95% CI: [” << mean - margin << ”, ” << mean + margin << ”] ms”;

8.  WHAT DOES 1.96 MEAN?

For a normal distribution:

approximately 68% -> mean ± 1 standard deviation approximately 95% ->
mean ± 1.96 standard deviations approximately 99.7% -> mean ± 3 standard
deviations

For the confidence interval of the mean, we apply 1.96 to the Standard
Error:

mean ± 1.96 * SE

NOTE: With only 10 samples and a standard deviation estimated from those
same samples, a more rigorous statistical analysis would normally use
Student’s t-distribution rather than 1.96.

Our benchmark uses 1.96 as a simple approximation for understanding the
concept.

9.  MINIMUM AND MAXIMUM

Minimum is the smallest measured execution time.

Maximum is the largest measured execution time.

In this example:

Min = 9 ms Max = 11 ms

They provide a quick view of the extremes, but they do not describe how
all measurements are distributed.

10. SUMMARY OF THE EXAMPLE

Samples: 9, 10, 11, 10, 10, 9, 11, 10, 9, 11 ms

Approximate results:

N = 10 Mean = 10.000 ms Min = 9.000 ms Max = 11.000 ms Variance = 0.600
ms^2 Standard Deviation = 0.775 ms Standard Error = 0.245 ms 95% CI =
[9.520, 10.480] ms

11. KEY IDEA FOR PROFILING AND BENCHMARKING

A useful way to remember these concepts is:

MEASUREMENTS | +–> Mean | What is the average execution time? | +–>
Variance / Standard Deviation | How much do individual executions vary?
| +–> Standard Error | How precisely have we estimated the mean? | +–>
95% Confidence Interval What interval represents the uncertainty of that
mean?

Performance decisions should not be based on a single execution.

For example:

Version A: 10.0 ms Version B: 9.8 ms

A difference of 0.2 ms may not represent a real performance improvement
if the measurements have significant variability.

Repeated measurements, standard deviation, Standard Error, and
confidence intervals help distinguish a consistent performance
improvement from normal measurement noise.
